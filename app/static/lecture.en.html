From search to connections:
an introduction to GraphRAG

RAG finds "sentences similar to the question". But what if the answer is spread across several chapters, and the pieces are linked only by relationships? Today we compare vector RAG and GraphRAG hands-on, using the Bank of Korea report Innovation and Economic Growth: Review of Korean Firms' Innovation Performance (May 2024, English edition, 48 pages).

Vector RAG · today's experiment

"What financing problem do young SMEs face, and where does Korea rank in access to the funding source blamed for it?"

It finds the Chapter II document on financing difficulties (doc09), but misses the Chapter III document (doc11) that says venture capital accessibility ranks 24th in the OECD. Instead, a different ranking story, "Korean business R&D ranks 2nd in the OECD" (doc03), shows up. 1 of 2 required facts (k=3, simple embedding).

It follows the path between the two entities in the question and collects evidence from both Chapter II (the problem) and Chapter III (the cause). 2 of 2 required facts (k=3).

Part 1 · Basics

What is a graph?

A graph is a data structure made of nodes (points) and the edges (lines) that connect them. Subway maps, social networks and web links are all graphs. The key idea is that a graph records not just "what exists" but "what is connected to what".

Try it: click two people in turn to find the shortest path
Click a start nodeHover over a node to highlight its neighbors (1-hop).

Nodes · edges

Node = an entity (person, company, product). Edge = a relationship between two of them. The number of edges attached to a node is its degree.

Direction · labels

Edges can have a direction, like "basic research → patent citations", and a name (label) such as IMPROVES that carries meaning.

Paths · hops

A route along edges is a path. One step is one hop. Multi-hop reasoning = searching for paths in a graph.

Knowledge graphs

Triples (RDF style)

Every piece of knowledge is a three-part fact: (subject, predicate, object).

(basic research, IMPROVES, patent citations)
(large firms, SUFFERS, stagnant innovation quality)
(secondary market, ENABLES, VC accessibility)

Property graphs

Nodes and edges can carry properties (key-value pairs). Graph databases like Neo4j and most GraphRAG tools use this model.

(:Policy {name:"stronger industry-academia collaboration"})
  -[:RAISES {growth:"+0.18%p", source:"doc16"}]->
(:Outcome {name:"economic growth rate"})

A predefined list of allowed node and relationship types is called a schema or ontology. With a schema, extraction is consistent; without one, it is flexible but messy. This trade-off comes back later as a central GraphRAG issue.

Three kinds of questions vector RAG is structurally weak at

① Multi-hop

"Which R&D type is linked to innovation quality, and how much would supporting it together with public research institutes raise growth?" The bridging concept (basic research) is not in the question, and the evidence is split between Chapter III (evidence) and Chapter IV (scenarios).

② Global summary

"What is the overall structure of this report and its risk factors?" A few top-k chunks cannot see the whole corpus. Raising k overflows the context.

③ Relations and aggregation

"List every policy that raises social welfare." Similarity cannot express the type and direction of relationships, exhaustive collection, or counting.

Part 2 · Concepts

Why GraphRAG emerged

The report is written in the order problems (Ch. II) → causes (early Ch. III) → evidence (Ch. III) → policy effects (Ch. IV) → prescriptions (Ch. V). Even the facts about "SME financing" alone are scattered across chapters.

doc09 · Ch. IIYoung SMEs citing lack of external funds: 9.9% → 45.4%
→
doc10 · Ch. IIICause: weak supply of innovation funding from venture capital
→
doc11 · Ch. IIIVC accessibility 24th in the OECD, government-led VC
→
doc13 · Ch. IIIWorks only with a developed secondary market (M&A, IPO)
→
doc18 · Ch. VPrescription: develop the M&A market, expand independent and corporate VC

From a chunk's point of view

The "24th" chunk in doc11 never mentions "young SMEs". Because its wording does not overlap with the question, vector search pushes it down and instead ranks an unrelated chunk (business R&D ranking, doc03) that shares words like "Korea", "OECD" and "rank (2nd)".

From the graph's point of view

Linking the relations each document leaves behind (suffers, weakness leads to, measures, enables) gives a 3-hop path from young SMEs to VC accessibility. The source documents of the relations on that path are exactly the evidence we need.

The core idea of GraphRAG: "read the documents in advance and store their relationships as structure; at question time, follow the structure to gather evidence." The expensive understanding step (extraction) happens at indexing time, so query time only needs fast traversal.

Part 2 · Concepts

How does GraphRAG work?

Indexing: building the graph

Split into chunkssame as vector RAG
→
Extract entities and relationsLLM extracts triples per chunk (structured output)
→
Entity resolutionmerge the same entity, e.g. "VC" and "venture capital"
→
Store the graphnetworkx, Neo4j, ...
→
Detect and summarize communitiescluster with Leiden, summarize with an LLM (MS GraphRAG)

Querying: gathering evidence through the graph

Local Search

Find the entities in the question (entity linking), then collect their neighbors, connecting paths and source text. Used for questions about specific entities or relations.

Entity linking→Expand neighbors · paths→Rank by importance (PPR)→Triples + source text

Global Search

Send the question to every precomputed community summary to get partial answers (Map), then combine the highest-scoring ones (Reduce). Used for "overall themes and structure" questions.

N summaries→ Map →partial answers + scores→ Reduce →final answer

DRIFT Search

Uses Global to set the overall direction, then generates follow-up questions and digs in with Local. A hybrid mode Microsoft GraphRAG added in late 2024.

Text2Cypher

The LLM translates natural language into a graph query language (Cypher) and runs it directly on the database. Strong at aggregation ("how many?") and exact filters.

Is machine-generating a graph with an LLM appropriate? Six perspectives

① Accuracy: invented and missing relations

LLMs sometimes produce plausible relations that are not in the document (hallucination), and often miss relations spanning several sentences or figures in tables and footnotes. Errors in the graph propagate into retrieval and answers, and retrieval cannot tell that they are errors.

② Reproducibility: temperature 0 does not guarantee identical output

Temperature 0 only reduces randomness; results can still change with server-side parallelism or model updates. Many recent reasoning models do not accept temperature at all. Reproducibility is more realistically achieved with structured output (JSON schema) + saved result snapshots + pinned model versions.

③ Lost meaning: an economic report's hedges and conditions disappear

The report modulates its claims with phrases like "may", "significant only where the secondary market is developed" and "model estimates". A triple like (venture capital, CAUSES, financing difficulty) erases that nuance and makes estimates and correlations look like established causation. Relations should keep confidence, conditions and evidence sentences as properties.

④ Consistency: relation and entity names drift

In free-form extraction the same relation is split into CAUSES, LEADS_TO and RESULTS_IN, and the same entity into "venture capital", "VC" and "venture investment". A fragmented graph breaks path search. That is why schema-guided extraction and entity resolution are essential.

⑤ Verifiability and accountability

If the graph feeds policy decisions or course material, every relation needs a source document and sentence so people can trace it. If a full review is impractical, review a random sample and report precision. Also check whether sending documents to an external API is allowed (copyright, security).

⑥ Cost and scale

A 48-page report is about 200 chunks, which is cheap, but with tens of thousands of documents extraction costs and time reach dozens of times those of vector indexing. That is why LazyGraphRAG and LightRAG appeared. Conversely, for small, critical document sets, the most efficient approach is a human-designed schema, an LLM draft, and human review.

Part 2 · Concepts

Vector RAG vs GraphRAG: what is different?

They are less competitors than two different retrieval criteria. Vector RAG asks "is it similar in meaning?"; GraphRAG asks "is it structurally connected?".

Common misconception: "GraphRAG is always better than vector RAG" is not true. Several benchmark studies found vector RAG equal or better on simple factual questions, with the graph advantage concentrated on multi-hop and global questions. The practical standard is a hybrid of both. Let's check it ourselves in the experiments below.

Observation ① On a single report, vector search is already strong

Because the questions share a lot of wording with the report, vector search already reaches a high average recall (0.79–0.93 at k=5, depending on the embedding model). "GraphRAG always wins" is not true.

Observation ② Where the graph wins

Questions like q3, where the evidence linking two concepts in the question lives in another chapter. While vector search is pulled toward the words "Korea · OECD · rank" and picks an unrelated chunk (R&D ranking), the graph reaches the Chapter III document through a path.

Observation ③ Where both miss

Phrases like "the types the conclusion recommends" in q5, which point to a place in the document rather than naming an entity. This is a limit of string-matching entity linking, and why an that interprets the question is needed.

Limits of this experiment

One report (about 200 chunks) is small. The reference graph was curated from key relations and checked against the source, so it is cleaner than automatic extraction (see the "LLM graph extraction" section). Results vary with the embedding model, k and chunk size; try changing them yourself.

Part 4 · Evolution

How GraphRAG evolved

Each stage appeared to solve a problem left by the previous one. Read it as a chain of problem → solution → remaining challenge.

~2023 · prehistory

Knowledge graphs + language models

There was a long line of research on question answering over structured KGs such as Wikidata (KGQA).

Limit KGs had to be built by hand, which was expensive and disconnected from unstructured text such as internal documents
2020

RAG (Lewis et al., Facebook AI)

A framework where the LLM retrieves external documents to answer. The prototype of the vector RAG taught in this course.

Remaining chunk-level similarity search is weak at multi-hop and global questions
2023

Think-on-Graph (ToG)

The LLM explores a KG like an agent with beam search, choosing which relation to follow next.

Contribution "exploring while understanding relations"Limit needs a well-built KG, many LLM calls
2024.01

RAPTOR

Recursively clusters and summarizes chunks into a tree. Not a graph, but it showed the idea of "answering global questions with hierarchical summaries".

2024.02

G-Retriever · GNN-based methods

Uses graph neural networks (GNNs) to select a relevant subgraph and pass it to the LLM. GNN-RAG (2024.05) and others followed.

Limit needs training data and model training, hard to adopt in practice
2024.04 → 07

Microsoft GraphRAG ("From Local to Global")

An LLM builds the KG from documents automatically, pre-generates a summary for each Leiden community, and answers global questions with Global Search. Its open-source release in July started the "GraphRAG" boom.

Solved the (human) cost of building KGs, global questionsRemaining very high LLM indexing cost, recomputation when documents are added
2024.05

HippoRAG

Inspired by the hippocampal memory theory of the brain. Runs Personalized PageRank over an extracted KG to find multi-hop evidence in a single retrieval. The ranking in today's Lab 04 uses this idea.

2024.09

KAG (Ant Group)

Knowledge-augmented generation based on schemas and logical forms, aimed at professional domains such as finance and healthcare where accuracy matters.

2024.10

LightRAG · DRIFT Search

LightRAG skips community summaries and does dual-level retrieval over entities (low level) and topic keywords (high level), with incremental updates, making it lightweight. Around the same time Microsoft released DRIFT Search, which bridges Global and Local.

Solved cost, updates
2024.11

LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft)

Does not precompute summaries and defers LLM processing until query time. The goal is to bring indexing cost down to the level of vector RAG.

2025.01

Zep · Graphiti: time-aware knowledge graphs

Each relation has a validity period; when a contradicting new fact arrives, the old one is invalidated. An example of GraphRAG expanding into long-term memory for agents.

2025

HippoRAG 2 · PathRAG · NodeRAG · HyperGraphRAG

Research continued on refining retrieval quality and expressiveness: path-level pruning (PathRAG), heterogeneous node design (NodeRAG), hyperedges linking three or more entities (HyperGraphRAG).

2025

Evaluation and skepticism: GraphRAG-Bench and others

Researchers began to measure systematically "when does the graph actually win?". Results repeatedly showed the benefit concentrated on multi-hop and global questions, with little gain on simple ones.

2025 → 2026

Agentic · hybrid · schema-first

Instead of fixed pipelines, LLM agents choose graph tools (e.g. MCP servers), and vector + keyword + graph hybrids became standard. Enterprises increasingly returned to schema-first extraction. MS GraphRAG reached version 3.x.

Part 5 · Today

Recent trends and open problems (2025–2026)

Cutting cost: Lazy · Lite

Defer processing to query time (LazyGraphRAG), skip summaries (LightRAG), or use small extraction-only models. Incremental indexing has become the default.

Agentic GraphRAG

An agent repeatedly picks tools such as search_entity, get_neighbors, find_path and run_cypher. Exposing a graph DB as an MCP server has become a common pattern.

Agent memory = time-aware KG

Graphiti/Zep, Mem0, Cognee and others extract facts from conversations into a graph and resolve contradictions with validity periods.

Hybrid is the standard

Vector (meaning) + BM25 (keywords) + graph expansion (relations) → reranker. Graph DBs with built-in vector indexes are now common.

The return of schemas and ontologies

Free-form extraction is noisy, so enterprises define a domain schema first and extract only within it.

More expressive graphs

Expanding to n-ary relations (hypergraphs), table and image nodes (multimodal), and call graphs of code repositories (context for coding agents).

Wrap-up

When should you use GraphRAG?

SituationRecommendation
Simple FAQ, fact lookupVector RAG + reranker is enough
Frequent multi-hop or causal-chain questionsGraph path and PPR expansion + vector hybrid (HippoRAG 2, LightRAG)
"Overall summary / themes" questionsGlobal Search over community summaries (MS GraphRAG, LazyGraphRAG)
Clear domain schema (finance, manufacturing)Schema-based KG + Neo4j + Text2Cypher
Documents and facts keep changingIncremental update tools (LightRAG, Graphiti)
Long-term agent memoryTime-aware KG (Graphiti/Zep, Mem0)

Key takeaways

  • Graph = nodes and edges. Multi-hop reasoning = path search
  • GraphRAG extracts relations at indexing time and follows the structure to gather evidence at query time
  • Strengths: multi-hop and global questions. Weaknesses: extraction cost and quality
  • In practice: hybrid, and expanding into agents and memory
